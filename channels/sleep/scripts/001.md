## Dusk on the Village Lane

Imagine a winter afternoon in the English Midlands, somewhere around the year thirteen hundred. The light has already begun to thin, even though the day was never very bright. In the middle of winter, in this part of the world, daylight lasts only a little under eight hours, and by the middle of the afternoon the sky has turned the colour of pewter, and the shadows of the bare trees have stretched out long across the frozen ground.

You are standing at the edge of a village. There is nothing to hurry you, so you can simply look. A single lane runs through the middle of the settlement, its surface a patchwork of hard ruts and frozen puddles, the mud of the autumn locked solid by the frost. Along either side of the lane stand the houses, low and close to the ground, with steep roofs of thatch that come down nearly to the height of your shoulder at the eaves. Thin ribbons of smoke are rising from them, and in the still, cold air they climb almost straight up before they fade into the grey.

Each house stands on its own narrow plot of land, which the people of the village call a toft, and behind each house there is usually a little patch of ground, a croft, where a family might grow cabbages and onions, leeks and beans, and perhaps keep a few hens. Between the plots there are hedges of thorn, or fences of woven hazel, or simply a ditch. Everything is made from what the land itself provides, so that the village looks less like something built and more like something that grew there.

Beyond the last of the houses, the fields open out. They do not look the way fields look today. There are no neat hedges dividing them into squares. Instead, a vast stretch of open land lies spread out in front of you, and it is ploughed into long, narrow strips, hundreds of them, each belonging to one family or another, scattered here and there so that everyone has a share of the good soil and a share of the poor. In winter the strips lie bare and brown and stiff with frost. The great labours of the ploughing and the sowing are behind the village now, and the harvest is long since in. For a few months, the land is allowed to rest, and so are most of the people who work it.

Down at the lower end of the village a stream runs beside the lane, and a small pond lies near the green, its surface covered with a thin skin of ice. Beside the pond a few geese are walking slowly in a line, as if they too have come to the end of the day. Nearby stands the church, the only building made largely of stone, with a squat tower, and close to it the manor house, the larger building that belongs to the lord of the estate, who may be seldom seen here. Neither of them has any need of you tonight. You can let them be.

Now people begin to appear, one at a time, coming home along the lane. A man walks past carrying a flail over his shoulder, the long wooden tool used for threshing grain, and he is breathing out small clouds of white as he goes. He has spent the day in a barn, beating the stored sheaves on the floor to shake loose the grain, a slow and steady kind of work that fills many of the short days of winter. Behind him comes a woman with a bundle of sticks on her back, gathered from along the hedgerow, and a child trotting beside her with a smaller bundle of his own, proud to be helping.

Nobody here owns a clock. There is not a single clock in the village, and nobody feels the lack of one. The day is measured by the sun, and when the sun is hidden, by the rhythm of the work, and by the church bell, which rings at certain hours to call people to prayer. In the winter, when the light goes early, the day simply ends earlier. There is no argument about it. When it is too dark to see the work in front of you, the work is done.

So the village begins to close itself up for the night. A gate swings shut with a low creak. Somewhere a bucket knocks against the stones of a well. A dog barks once, thinks better of it, and falls quiet. A voice calls a child in from the lane, not loudly, only the long, familiar call of someone who has made it a thousand times. And one by one, the small orange glows of fires begin to show through the gaps around the wooden shutters, like the first stars appearing in a darkening sky.

You can follow the lane a little further now, and choose one of these houses to step into out of the cold. Let us go to the one near the middle, where a thread of smoke is rising a little thicker than the others, and where the door is only half closed against the wind.

## The House Beside the Lane

The house is smaller than you might expect, and it feels older than the people inside it. It belongs to a family that is reasonably comfortable by the standards of the village, because the poorest cottagers live in much smaller and simpler buildings than this one, and it is worth keeping that in mind as we look around. It has a frame of heavy timbers, cut from the oaks of the nearby woodland, and in many houses of this region the frame is built around pairs of great curved timbers, called crucks, that rise from the ground to the top of the roof. Between the timbers, the walls are filled with a woven lattice of thin hazel rods, and over the lattice has been spread a thick plaster made of clay, straw, and a little dung, which has dried to a pale, rough finish. This kind of wall is called wattle and daub, and in other parts of the country, where stone is plentiful or clay is of a different sort, the walls might instead be made of stone, or of packed earth. Here, in the Midlands, a house like this is common.

The roof is thatch, a deep layer of straw or reed laid thickly over a frame of rafters. It is a wonderful thing in winter, because it holds a great deal of air within it, and air that is held still is a good barrier against the cold. It is also home, as every thatch has always been, to a small and busy community of mice and sparrows, who go about their business in the layers above the family's heads and are, for the most part, politely ignored.

You duck a little as you pass through the doorway, because the door is low, and when you straighten up you find that the interior is a single large room, or perhaps one large room and a smaller one divided off at the far end by a screen of woven rods or a hanging cloth. The floor is made of packed earth, swept hard and smooth by years of feet. There is no ceiling, so that when you look up you can see the underside of the thatch and the dark, smoke-stained curves of the rafters. The windows are no more than small openings in the walls, closed against the weather with wooden shutters, because glass is a costly thing, found in churches and the houses of the wealthy, and it would never occur to anyone here to expect it in a farmhouse.

Because the windows are small and shuttered, the room is dim, and it would be dim even in daylight. The eye needs a moment to adjust. Gradually, as it does, you begin to make out the shapes of things. A long wooden table stands on trestles to one side, so that it can be taken down and moved when the room is needed for something else. A couple of stools and a bench are drawn up near the middle of the floor. Along one wall there is a chest, bound with iron straps, where the family keeps its few valuable things: the better clothes, a little linen, perhaps a small bag of coins that has been saved for some purpose. On pegs driven into the beams hang cloaks and hoods, a basket, a sieve, and a bundle of dried herbs, and from a rafter hangs a flitch of bacon, cured and smoked in the autumn and now carefully rationed.

There is a particular smell to the room, and it is hard to describe if you have never known it. It is the smell of woodsmoke, first of all, which has soaked into the beams and the thatch and the clothes over many years, but under it there is the smell of wool, of damp earth, of the dried herbs and the cooking, and a faint warm scent of animals, because in winter some of the livestock may be brought close for warmth. It is not an unpleasant smell. To the people who live here, it is simply the smell of home.

The house is not large, but it is made to hold a good deal of life. A family of five or six people might live here, a husband and wife, their children, and perhaps an elderly parent, and there may be a servant or a lodger as well. Every part of the room has more than one use. The table is a table at mealtimes and a workbench in the afternoon. The bench by the fire is a seat in the evening and a bed for the youngest child later on. The chest is a store, a seat, and sometimes a place to lay out clean linen. Nothing is wasted, because there is not enough of anything to waste, and the result, strangely, is a kind of calm, a feeling that everything has its place because it has to.

Look at the way the family moves about the room. They do not hurry. They have all spent the day in the cold, and the warmth of the house is something they are taking in slowly, the way you might sip something hot. The woman of the house is crouched near the hearth, and she is the one who sets the tempo of the evening. She is tending the fire, and the fire is the heart of this house and of every house like it. Let us come closer, and sit for a while where it is warm.

## The Hearth and the Light

The hearth is not set against a wall, as it would be in a later age. It sits roughly in the middle of the floor, a simple, shallow patch of flat stones or hardened clay, perhaps surrounded by a low kerb to keep the embers from creeping outward. A chimney, in the sense that we would understand it, does not exist here. Chimneys will come to ordinary houses in the centuries ahead, but at this time the smoke finds its own way up through the open space beneath the roof, drifts among the rafters, and slips out through the thatch, or through a small opening left near the peak. This is why the rafters are dark and glossy with soot, and why the smoke hangs in a soft, slow layer above your head, and why the family has learned to sit low, where the air is clearer.

The fire itself is small, and it is a careful thing. Wood is not as abundant as you might imagine, and the gathering of it was often governed by local custom, so that a family would take care to use only what it was allowed to take. So the woman builds her fire with patience, a few split logs, a handful of kindling, and a heap of dried furze or twigs for a quick bright flame when she needs it. She nudges a log into place with her foot, and sparks lift and fade, and a small, steady heat begins to move out into the room.

Around the fire, the shape of the evening comes together. The cooking pot hangs from an iron hook or a chain suspended from a beam, and beneath it the flames are kept low, so the contents simmer rather than boil. A flat iron plate, or a griddle, leans against the stones, ready for baking. A few simple pots of clay sit nearby, glazed on the inside, with the soot of long use blackening their sides. Nothing here is fine. But everything is kept clean, and well used, and treasured.

Light in the house comes almost entirely from the fire. When the flames burn low, the room settles into shadows, and the shapes of the people become soft and rounded, their faces lit gold on one side and lost in darkness on the other. But the family has other ways to make light, and the simplest of them is the rushlight.

A rushlight is a simple, cheap light that ordinary households in Britain used for many centuries. Most of the descriptions we have of it come from later periods, but it is very likely that something much like it lit houses such as this one. It is made from the common rush that grows in wet ground along the edges of ponds and streams. The rushes are gathered in the summer, and the outer green skin is carefully peeled away, leaving a narrow strip so that the pale pith inside is held together. The pith is then dried, and dipped in melted fat, saved from the cooking, until it has taken up a thin coating. When the coating hardens, you have a slender, pale stick, rather like a thin candle, and when you light one end it burns with a small yellow flame, giving off a modest light and a faint smell of burnt fat. It does not last long. A rushlight burns away fairly quickly, and so a household might light only one at a time, and only when there is a real need, to look for something in the chest, or to see to a sick animal, or to find the way across the yard.

A little further up the scale there is the tallow candle, which is made by dipping a wick repeatedly into melted animal fat until it builds up into a candle shape. Tallow candles give a better light and last longer, but they are smoky and smell strongly, and for many ordinary people they were a costly thing. A family in a village like this one might use them only for special occasions, or when there is some task that needs close attention, the mending of a tool, or the sewing of a seam. And beyond them are the candles of beeswax, which burn cleanly and sweetly, and cost a good deal more. The church required beeswax candles for the Mass, and few people could afford to burn them at home. In this house, you would be unlikely to see one.

All of this means that evenings here are shaped by the light in a way that is hard for us to picture. When the fire is bright, the family is awake, and the work of the evening goes on. When the fire sinks to its embers, and the single flame of the rushlight is pinched out, the room belongs to the darkness, and the people in it begin, quite naturally, to think about sleep. There is no glowing screen, no bright lamp, no switch to flick. The end of the day is announced not by a clock or a decision, but simply by the slow fading of the light, and the body follows.

The woman reaches for another log, and sets it gently across the embers. The flames flicker, and settle, and the smell of fresh woodsmoke rises, warm and resinous, and mixes with the scent of the pot hanging above. Somewhere in the thatch, a mouse shifts, and is still. The family is gathering, one by one, close to the fire. And on the hook above the flames, the evening meal is almost ready.

## Supper by the Fire

The main meal of the day, in a house like this, was eaten earlier, somewhere between the middle of the morning and midday, and it was called dinner. Exactly when people ate varied, and the evidence is not precise. The evening meal is lighter, and it has its own name, supper, and it is often made from what remains of the pot that has been simmering since the morning. That pot is the quiet centre of the family's food, and it is called pottage.

Pottage is a thick, slow stew, and it can hold almost anything. At its base are grains and pulses, oats or barley, and dried peas or beans, which have been soaked and then cooked down until they are soft and heavy. To these the woman adds whatever the garden and the store can offer in the middle of winter: onions, leeks, perhaps a few cabbage leaves from the plot behind the house, a handful of dried herbs from the bundle on the beam. If the family has any scrap of bacon or a bone to spare, it goes in too, and gives the pottage a savoury depth. If not, the pottage is simply green and gold and good, and it is eaten with gratitude all the same.

Beside the pottage, there is bread. The bread here is not the pale, soft loaf of later centuries. It is dark and coarse, made from barley, or from rye, or from a mixture of wheat and rye that is sometimes called maslin, depending on what the fields have given that year and what the family can afford. It has a thick, chewy crust and a dense crumb, and it is the thing that fills people up. Some of it is baked on the household's own hearth or griddle, and in places where there is a communal oven, or one belonging to the lord of the manor, a family might pay a small fee to use it. Practice varied from one village to the next. In this house a loaf sits on the table, torn at one end, and the family will tear off pieces as they eat.

There is also cheese, hard and a little sharp, kept in a cool corner and cut in thin slices, and a few eggs, although the hens lay fewer of them in winter, when the days are short, so each egg is something of a small event. And there is ale. Ale is the ordinary drink of the household, brewed from malted barley, and in this period it was made without hops, which meant that it did not keep for long and was best drunk fairly soon after it was brewed. Historians disagree about just how quickly it spoiled. Most of the brewing in medieval England was done by women, who were known as alewives or brewsters, and a woman who had a fresh batch to sell might set out a pole or a branch by her door to let her neighbours know. For a family like this one, the ale is a daily comfort, poured from a jug into wooden cups and passed around the table.

Notice how simple the table is. The bowls and cups are mostly of wood, worn smooth by long use, and the spoons are of wood or of horn. Most people carry a small knife of their own at the belt, used for everything from cutting cheese to mending a strap. There are no forks. There are no plates in the way we would think of them, but a few of the family share a larger wooden dish, or a slice of old bread called a trencher serves as a plate, and is eaten at the end of the meal when it has soaked up the last of the gravy.

Before anyone eats, there is a short prayer, spoken quietly, and the words are the same ones every family in the village has said for generations. The man of the house says it, and the others murmur their response. Then the bowls are filled and passed along, and for a while there is little to be heard in the room but the soft sound of spoons against wood, and the crackle of the fire, and the occasional low remark about the day.

Conversation at supper is gentle and unhurried. Someone mentions that the old ox seems a little stiff in the cold, and someone else says that the frost will be good for the ground, breaking up the heavy clods in the fields. One of the children asks a question about the church bell, and is answered, patiently, by a grandmother. A neighbour is mentioned, who has a cough, and the woman says she will take her a little of the pottage tomorrow. These are not important matters, but they are the web of small concerns that holds a village together, and they are discussed with the kind of attention that comes from knowing every person involved, and knowing that you will see them every day for the rest of your lives.

When the meal is finished, there is no great clearing away. The bowls are wiped with the last of the bread, and set aside to be scrubbed in the morning. The scraps are saved for the hens and the pig. The woman stirs the pot one last time, and moves it a little to the side of the heat, where it will keep warm and settle into something a little thicker by the morning. The man leans back against the wall and stretches his legs towards the fire, and lets out a long, slow breath. The day's work is done, and the evening has begun.

## The Last Tasks of the Day

Before the family can truly settle, there are a few small things still to be done, and they are done without fuss, because they are done every night. The first of these is to see to the animals.

In a village like this, a family's animals are a large part of its wealth and its survival. A household of moderate means might keep a cow, a few sheep, a pig or two, and a flock of hens and geese, and some of the richer villagers may own an ox or two for the plough. Through the summer, the animals graze on the common pasture and the meadow, and in the autumn the pigs are turned out into the woods to feed on acorns. But now it is winter, and the grass has stopped growing, and fodder is precious. Hay and straw have been stacked and stored, and every handful is counted, because it must last until spring.

Many of the animals were killed in the autumn, around the feast of Martinmas, in the middle of November, because there would not be enough feed to keep them all through the cold months. The meat was salted, or smoked, and hung from the rafters, which is why a flitch of bacon hangs in the house tonight. What remains are the animals that are most useful alive: the cow for milk, the sheep for wool, the hens for eggs, and the oxen for the spring ploughing.

The man of the house puts on his cloak, and steps out into the yard. The cold takes him at once, and the frozen ground rings softly under his boots. He checks that the gate of the pen is fastened, and that the hens have gone up to their roost, and he counts them quietly, as he does each night. He looks in at the cow, which stands in her shelter, steaming faintly in the cold air, and gives her a measured armful of hay, and pats her flank, and she lets out a long, low breath. He glances up at the sky, where the stars are beginning to show, sharp and white and countless, with none of the glare of modern lights to dim them. Then he goes back inside, and fastens the door.

The second task of the evening is to put the fire to bed. A thatched house is a wonderful thing, but it is also a great danger when the fire is unattended, because a single spark on a dry roof can spread through a whole village in the space of an hour. For this reason, villages, and especially towns, have long had rules about fire at night. In some places a bell was rung in the evening as a signal that every household should cover its fire, and the word curfew is thought to come from the old French expression for exactly that, a call to cover the fire. How strictly the rule was followed in a small English village is harder to say, but the habit of banking the fire for the night was certainly a common one.

The woman does this with practised care. She rakes the glowing coals together into a small, tidy heap at the centre of the hearth, and covers them with a layer of ash, so that the embers beneath will smoulder slowly through the night, giving off a little warmth, and be ready to be woken in the morning with a few dry twigs and a breath. The room dims at once, and the flames that had been dancing a moment ago are gone. In their place there is a low, red glow, and the smell of ash and smoke, and a hush that seems to settle over the house.

Somewhere across the village, a bell begins to ring. It is not the loud clanging of a great alarm, but a measured, mellow sound, one note after another, carried across the fields on the cold air. It marks the end of the day for the people who can hear it, and it reaches the house through the thatch and the timbers like a soft pulse. The family pause and listen, as they always do, and perhaps one of them murmurs a short prayer. And then the sound fades away into the stillness, and the night is properly begun.

## Evening by the Embers

Banked fire or not, the family does not go to bed the moment the light goes. There is a period of time between the last chores and the last sleep, and it is one of the most pleasant parts of the day. They gather close to the warm hearthstones, on stools and on the bench, with their cloaks around their shoulders, and they let the evening unfold.

There is always a little work to be done by touch and by firelight. The woman has taken up her spindle, a small weighted stick onto which she draws and twists wool from a bundle held on a distaff tucked under her arm, and the spindle turns and dips and turns again, spinning a fine, even thread out of the soft grey fleece. She does not need to look closely at it. Her hands know the motion so well that she can talk, or listen, or sing, while the thread lengthens in her fingers. Spinning is a task that very many women did in their spare moments, because wool is the great product of the country, and the thread she makes tonight will be woven into cloth for the family, or sold, to help pay the rent.

Her husband, meanwhile, is mending. A leather strap lies across his knee, and he is working an awl through it with slow, steady pulls, binding a new piece to an old harness. Beside him, the eldest son is carving a spoon from a block of pale wood, taking off thin curls with his knife, and letting them fall at his feet to be swept into the fire in the morning. The smallest child has fallen half asleep against her grandmother's side, and the grandmother, who has been quiet for some time, is humming a little tune that seems to have no beginning and no end.

People talk, but not about the kinds of things that fill a modern evening. They talk about the weather, and what the frost will do to the fields. They talk about the neighbour whose cow is due to calve, and the man in the next village who has a good bull. They remember things that happened in the past, in great detail, because the memory of a village is long and everyone has a share in it. They recall who the boundary stone belonged to, and which year the stream flooded, and what the old priest said at the burial of the miller's wife. Stories are passed from the old to the young as naturally as bread is passed around the table, and a child who sits long enough by the fire will absorb the history of an entire community without ever being taught it.

Some of the stories are tales of saints, told in a plain way, with a gentle moral. Some are older and stranger, stories of giants and wells and hidden treasure in the hills, handed down so long that nobody remembers who first told them. Some are simply riddles and sayings, the short, memorable phrases that people of every age have used to pass on what they know about weather and work and the habits of neighbours. We do not know most of the words that were spoken in rooms like this one, because almost none of it was written down, but we can be fairly sure that the children chimed in with the endings they knew, and that the elders corrected them, smiling.

If there is a little energy left, there might be a game. Boards for a game called nine men's morris have been found cut into the stone seats of cloisters at several English cathedrals and abbeys, and the game was known across Europe, so it would be perfectly natural to imagine it being played here, with pebbles for pieces and a pattern of squares scratched onto a flat piece of board. The game takes a long time to play, and nobody is in a hurry, and the pebbles click softly against the wood while the fire hisses and settles beneath the ash.

There is something in this slow evening that is hard to find in our own day, and it is not that the people are idle, because they are not. It is that nothing is competing for their attention. There is no screen, no notification, no sound from beyond the village. There is the fire, and the people in the room, and the dark outside, and the shared understanding that the day has been lived and is now closing. When the wool runs out on the distaff, when the spoon is finished, when the little ones can no longer keep their eyes open, someone yawns, and the yawn passes around the room, and it becomes quietly clear that it is time to go to bed.

## Going to Bed

Bedtime in this house is not a grand event. There is no bedroom, and no door to close, and no sound of water running in a bathroom, because there is none. There is only the warm room, the banked fire, and the soft business of arranging oneself for the night.

The beds are simple. For most families in a village like this, a bed is a mattress, or more exactly a sack, a large linen or canvas bag stuffed with straw, or with chaff, or with dried bracken, and laid down on the floor, or on a low frame of wood, or on a bench built against the wall. Better-off families might have a proper bedstead, with a frame and a cord laced across it to hold the mattress up off the cold ground, but in a modest house, the sack on the floor is the most common arrangement. The straw is fresh in the autumn, and by the middle of winter it has flattened and softened, and the family shakes it up, and adds a little new straw when they can.

Over the sack go the bedclothes, and wool is the great comfort here. There are blankets of coarse, warm wool, and coverlets, and perhaps an old sheepskin for the coldest nights. A family might own one or two linen sheets, kept carefully and washed rarely, or it might own none at all. Pillows are not common, but a rolled cloak, or a bolster stuffed with feathers or straw, will do for the head. Nobody here would think of any of this as hard, because it is what everybody has, and because there is nothing more satisfying than a woollen blanket on a very cold night.

The whole household sleeps in the same room, and this is not thought of as a hardship either, but as a sensible way to share warmth. The parents lie close together, the children are tucked in beside each other, and the grandmother takes the place nearest the hearth, where the warmth of the banked embers reaches her, as it should, because she is the oldest. The youngest child is lifted gently from the bench and carried to her bed, still half asleep, and laid down with a murmur, and covered over. We do not know exactly what each person wore in bed, because the evidence is scattered, but it is likely that most people slept in a linen shirt or undergarment, or in whatever they had worn beneath their clothes during the day, and that in the very coldest weather some of them kept on a layer or two of wool.

Before lying down, there is the matter of prayers. In the world of this village, almost everyone says them, and the words are the ones that many children were taught from an early age, often in Latin, and learned by heart, without necessarily understanding every phrase, because the sound itself is a comfort. The Lord's Prayer is spoken, and the Hail Mary, and a short blessing on the house and the people in it. The woman makes the sign of the cross over the door, over the embers, and over the sleeping children, a quiet, familiar gesture, and she does it in the dark without looking.

There is one last light, the single rushlight that has been burning in its holder near the table, and now it is time for it to go. The man pinches the flame between his finger and thumb, with the quick, practised touch of someone who has done it ten thousand times, and the room slips into darkness. For a moment you cannot see anything at all. Then, little by little, your eyes begin to understand what they are looking at. There is the dull red glow of the embers beneath the ash, a faint circle of warmth in the dark. There are the thin silver lines of moonlight slipping through the gaps in the shutters, laying pale stripes across the earthen floor. And there are the shapes of the sleepers, rising and falling, rising and falling, with the soft rhythm of breathing.

It is quiet now, but it is not silent. A quiet house is full of small sounds, and each of them is gentle. The thatch whispers faintly as the wind brushes across it. A timber ticks and settles as it cools. A blanket rustles as someone turns over. In the far corner, a child sighs in her sleep, and then, after a moment, is still. Outside, an animal shifts its weight in the straw, and the frost, creeping across the fields, makes the lightest sound of all, a thin, crisp creak, as the ground tightens in the cold.

The family, one by one, drifts away. They do not lie awake with worries about the morning, or with the glow of a screen still pressed into their eyes. They have worked with their bodies, they have eaten, they have been warm, and now the darkness comes in around them like another blanket. The mind lets go of the day gently, the way the hand lets go of a thread, and the breathing in the room slows and deepens, until the whole house seems to be breathing together.

## The First Sleep

There is a curious idea about how people in the past slept, and it is worth spending a little time on it, here in the quiet, because it may be the most interesting thing about the night in a village like this one. The historian Roger Ekirch spent many years searching through diaries, court records, medical books, and literature from the late Middle Ages and the centuries that followed, and he found hundreds of references to something that people called the first sleep, and the second sleep, or sometimes the morning sleep.

According to his research, people in many parts of Europe did not always sleep in a single unbroken stretch through the night. Instead, they would go to bed soon after dark, sleep for a few hours, and then wake up in the middle of the night for an hour or so, before drifting off into a second period of sleep that lasted until dawn. The time in between was a kind of quiet pause, and it seems to have been used in a number of ways. According to his reading of the evidence, some people lay still and prayed. Some talked quietly with whoever lay beside them. Some got up to add a log to the fire, or to check on the animals, or to use the chamber pot, or even to visit a neighbour who was also awake.

It is important to be honest about what we know. Ekirch's work has been widely read and discussed, and the references he found are real, but other historians have pointed out that the evidence is scattered and that it can be hard to tell how common the pattern really was, or how it varied from one person to another and one place to another. The question is still debated. But it is a gentle idea, and a rather lovely one, that the night once had two chapters, with a calm interval between them, rather than being a single block of time to be got through as efficiently as possible.

So perhaps, in this house, at some point in the deep middle of the night, one of the sleepers begins to stir. It might be the woman. She opens her eyes in the darkness, and for a moment she is not sure whether it is still dark because it is early or because it is late, and then she remembers, and she settles, and does not worry about it at all. She has no clock to consult, and nothing in particular to do. She lies still, and listens.

She hears the wind, which has risen a little since bedtime, and is pushing against the thatch with a long, soft sound like a sigh. She hears the embers in the hearth shift and settle with a tiny, dry whisper. Out in the yard, the cow breathes out, and something small and light runs across the roof. Far away, perhaps at the edge of the wood, an owl calls, a low, quavering note, and after a long pause, another answers from further off. The sounds come and go like a slow tide, and she does not try to follow any of them. They are simply the voice of the night, and she has heard it all her life.

Beside her, her husband breathes slowly and steadily. In the corner, the children are quiet. She thinks, perhaps, about nothing in particular, or about the day to come, or about the thread she will spin tomorrow, and the thoughts drift past like clouds without ever settling. Her mind is neither busy nor empty, but somewhere in between, in the soft, wandering state that comes in the small hours when the body is warm and the dark is kind. She might say a short prayer, or she might simply watch the faint red glow in the hearth, and let it hold her attention for a while.

And then, quite gradually, without any decision at all, the weight of sleep comes back. Her eyes close of their own accord. The sounds of the night begin to blur and recede, and the edges of her thoughts soften, and she slips down into the second sleep, deeper and slower than the first, and carries on towards the morning.

## Toward Morning

The night goes on, and there is nothing in it that needs your attention. Outside the house, the stars wheel slowly across the sky, as they have done every night since before there were villages to watch them. The great belt of the winter constellations climbs and passes, and the moon, which was low when the family went to bed, has slipped behind the church tower and out of sight. The cold deepens, as it always does in the hours before dawn, and a fine white frost forms on the thatch, on the fence rails, on the stiff brown grass of the croft.

Perhaps, a little after midnight, a few small flakes of snow begin to fall. They come very quietly, one at a time at first, and then in a slow, drifting curtain, settling on the roofs and the lane and the bare branches of the trees, and softening every edge. Snow makes the whole world quieter, and in a village that is already quiet it seems to lay a hush upon everything, so that even the wind seems to hold its breath. The footprints of the evening are filled in. The tracks of the dog by the gate disappear. By the end of the night, the lane will be a smooth white road with no marks on it at all.

Inside the house, nothing has changed. The embers glow faintly under their blanket of ash, giving off just enough warmth to take the edge from the air. The family sleeps, wrapped in wool, their breathing slow and even. The smallest child has rolled over, and one hand has crept out from under the blanket, and lies open on the straw, and the grandmother, without waking, reaches over and tucks it back in. It is the kind of small, unremarked kindness that happens a hundred times in the course of a family's life, and no one will remember it in the morning.

At some point, a long while from now, a cock will crow in the yard, a thin, clear sound in the grey half-light. Another will answer from a neighbour's croft, and then another, passing the news down the lane. The woman will stir, and rise, and wake the fire, and the day will begin again, with its familiar work, its barn and its flail, its spindle and its pot. The bell will ring for the morning. Smoke will rise from the roofs into the cold, clear air. But all of that belongs to the morning, and the morning is a long way off yet.

For now, there is only the night, and the snow, and the sleeping village. The fields lie white and still under the stars. The stream moves on beneath its thin ice, murmuring to itself. The church tower stands dark against the sky. In every house along the lane, people are lying in the warm, close dark, breathing in and out, one slow breath after another, and the thread of their sleep runs on, unbroken, from the end of one day towards the beginning of the next.
